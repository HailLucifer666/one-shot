### Manual 1: Porlex Mini Hand Coffee Grinder

**Overview.** The Porlex Mini is a ceramic-burr hand grinder sized for travel. It holds about 20 grams of whole beans and adjusts from Turkish-fine to French-press coarse via the ground adjuster under the handle. The ceramic burrs do not rust and add no metallic taste, and the stainless body survives being knocked around in a bag.

**Setup.** Unscrew the lid and remove the handle. Under the handle sits the spring, a washer, and the adjuster nut. Turn the adjuster nut clockwise for finer grounds and counter-clockwise for coarser; one full turn is a large change, so start with quarter turns. For espresso, begin two full turns clockwise from the loosest setting; for filter coffee, back off one and a half turns from there; for French press, go one further. Reassemble in reverse order: adjuster, washer, spring, handle, lid.

**Daily use.** Fill the hopper no higher than the fill line — overfilling makes the grind uneven and can crack the ceramic burrs if a stone hides in the beans. Grip the body firmly and turn the handle at a steady one revolution per second. An even feel with light resistance means the burrs are seated correctly; grinding should never require force. Grind directly into the catch cup, then tap twice before opening so grounds settle and don't puff out.

**Care and storage.** Empty the grounds completely after each use; oils left behind go rancid and spoil the next cup. Once a month, grind a small handful of raw rice to strip the oils, then brush out the dust with a dry toothbrush. Never wash the burr assembly with water — moisture swells the wood centre and cracks the ceramic. Wipe the exterior with a damp cloth and store the grinder empty with the lid off so residual moisture can escape.

**Troubleshooting.** If the handle spins freely with no grinding, the adjuster nut has backed off — disassemble and re-seat the spring and washer. If grinding suddenly requires force, a pebble is almost always lodged in the burrs; remove it by turning the handle backwards gently, never forwards. If grounds are uneven, check that you are not overfilling and that the handle is fully seated on its square shaft. A wobbly handle means the shaft was reassembled cross-threaded — back it off and rethread slowly. Care, done monthly, keeps this grinder working for a decade: rice-strip, brush, stay dry.

### Manual 2: Trailhead Pocket Camping Stove

**Overview.** The Trailhead Pocket is a canister-top stove weighing 73 grams, rated at 3.2 kilowatts. It screws onto standard threaded butane-propane canisters and folds to the size of a fist. The piezo igniter is built into the burner head, and the flame spreader is sized for pots from 12 to 22 centimetres.

**Setup.** Set the canister on flat, stable ground away from your tent and any overhang. Fold the three pot supports out until they click. Hold the stove upright and screw it onto the canister hand-tight — never with tools, and never while the flame is lit. Listen for the faint hiss stop, which means the seal is seated. Check the rubber O-ring on the stove's inlet thread before every trip; a cracked O-ring leaks.

**Daily use.** Open the gas valve a quarter turn, then press the igniter button two or three times. If it lights with a yellow, smoky flame, close the valve, wait a minute for gas to clear, and relight with a smaller opening — a lazy yellow flame means too much gas for the air supply. Simmer by turning the valve down until the flame tips just stop flickering; simmering is a valve skill, not a heat setting. In wind below 5 °C the canister chills and output drops — warm the canister in your jacket or set it in an inch of water to keep it above its boil point.

**Care and storage.** Always disconnect the stove from the canister before packing either one. Let the burner head cool for five minutes, then brush soot from the flame ports with the included needle. Never store the stove screwed onto a canister — vibration can open the valve. Keep the O-ring lightly coated with the silicone sachet, and pack the stove in its pouch away from sharp objects that could bend the flame tube.

**Troubleshooting.** If the igniter sparks but will not light, the spark gap has widened — bend the electrode wire 1 millimetre closer to the gas port. If the flame is low on a full canister, the canister is too cold, not empty. If you smell gas at any point, close the valve, move the canister outdoors, and soap-water test the inlet thread: bubbles mean a bad seal or a dead O-ring, and the stove must not be used until the ring is replaced. The day your flame turns lazy and yellow and stays that way, retire the stove — that is a blocked burner, not a valve problem.

### Manual 3: RiverFlow Squeeze Water Filter

**Overview.** The RiverFlow Squeeze is a hollow-fibre filter rated to 0.1 microns, which removes bacteria and protozoa but not viruses or chemicals. It flows about one litre per minute fresh and threads onto standard 28-millimetre soft bottles. Its weakness is freezing: ice inside the fibres cracks them invisibly, and a cracked filter filters nothing.

**Setup.** Screw the filter onto the supplied dirty-water bottle, or onto your squeeze bottle, hand-tight. Before the first use, backflush it with clean water: attach the syringe to the filter's outlet and push two full syringes of clean water backwards through the filter to wet the fibres and remove manufacturing dust. Prime by squeezing the dirty bottle until water runs clear from the outlet.

**Daily use.** Fill the dirty bottle from the clearest water you can find and pre-filter it through the bandana if it carries silt. Squeeze steadily; forcing it at maximum pressure just drives particles into the fibre walls. Keep the dirty side dirty: never touch the outlet thread to the water source, your fingers, or the ground — the outlet is your clean side, and one touch can recontaminate a litre. Collect into a clean container and drink or treat further if viruses are a concern.

**Care and storage.** Backflush after every trip: two syringes of clean water, backwards, then shake out and air-dry with the caps off for a full day. For long-term storage, sterilise by backflushing with a mild bleach solution (one drop per litre), then two syringes of clean water, then dry. Never let the filter freeze after it has been wetted — if a wetted filter has been below freezing, it is retired, no exceptions. Store it dry, capped, in its pouch.

**Troubleshooting.** Flow slowing to a trickle means the fibres are loaded — backflush until the runoff runs clear, which may take six to eight syringes after silty water. If backflushing does not restore flow, tap the outlet end against your palm while water is in it to dislodge clumps. A cracked fibre cannot be seen, but its sign is a filter that passes water freely yet tastes of the source; after any suspected freeze, the only safe assumption is failure. When the squeeze feels like pushing through sand even fresh from a backflush, the fibres are at end of life — retire the filter with a full day's drying and a note of its total litres.

### Manual 4: Summit 350 Headlamp

**Overview.** The Summit 350 is a 350-lumen headlamp with a red night mode, a lockout, and an IPX6 rating — it survives driving rain, not submersion. It runs on two AAA cells or the built-in 1000 mAh lithium pack, and switches sources automatically. Its real failure mode is not water: it is button drift inside a pack, draining the cell before you ever click it on.

**Setup.** Charge the lithium pack fully before the first trip — about two hours from empty, with the indicator turning green. To fit AAA cells, slide the battery tray out with your thumbnail and observe the polarity marks; mixing lithium and alkaline cells in the same tray is not supported and will trip the protection circuit. Adjust the strap so one finger fits under the front band; a loose strap is the main cause of beam wander on trail.

**Daily use.** Click once for the last-used brightness, click and hold to ramp up or down, double-click for red. Lock it out before packing: press and hold both buttons for three seconds until the lamp flashes twice — in lockout no button press will light it, which is the whole point. Use red mode around other people at camp; white light at 350 lumens ruins the night vision of everyone facing you and attracts every insect within thirty metres.

**Care and storage.** Rinse the strap in fresh water after sweaty trips and let it dry out of the sun. Wipe the lens with a soft cloth — headlamp lenses scratch easily and a scratched lens doubles the glare you see rather than the trail. Store at half charge if the lamp will sit unused for months, and never store it in lockout while the pack is deeply discharged; deep-discharged lithium cells degrade. Charge to full the night before every trip, not a week before.

**Troubleshooting.** If it will not charge, clean the USB port with a dry brush — trail dust packed into the port is the usual cause, not a dead cell. If it shuts off after a minute on high, the thermal guard is working: high output steps down in warm weather and this is by design, not a fault. If neither battery source lights the lamp, check the tray contacts for the faint white dust of a leaked alkaline cell; scrape it off with a wooden toothpick, never steel. When the lamp flashes three times and dims, the cell is at five percent — finish your task on red mode, which sips power. A headlamp kept locked out, charged, and dry is one you never think about; that is the goal.

### Manual 5: Atlas Floor Bike Pump

**Overview.** The Atlas is a steel-floor track pump with a 2.5-inch gauge, rated to 160 psi, with a dual head that grips both Presta and Schrader valves without adapters. The 32-centimetre barrel moves enough air to seat tubeless tyres. Its gauge is the part to trust once you learn its one habit: read the gauge while the tyre is connected, not after.

**Setup.** Bolt the stand feet on with the supplied hex key and snap the handle onto the rod. Flip the barrel latch and extend the barrel fully before the first stroke to check smooth travel — it should glide with no gritty spots. Select the valve port by flipping the dual head's lever to the side marked P or S; the wrong port presses the pin on the wrong valve and reads zero forever.

**Daily use.** Seat the head on the valve squarely, then pull the lever to lock. Pump with full strokes using your body weight, not your arms alone; the gauge needle should climb smoothly. Watch the gauge at the top of a stroke — that is the true tyre pressure. When you reach target pressure, hold the gauge steady, release the head lever with a quick firm pull, and pop the head off; slow releases hiss half your pressure back out. For tubeless seating, remove the valve core, pump fast with full strokes to 40 psi, then refit the core.

**Care and storage.** A drop of oil on the rod every couple of months keeps the seal supple; wipe excess off so it does not attract grit. Do not leave the pump in the sun with the hose under tension — the hose is the first part to fail and it fails where it meets the head. Store the handle down with the barrel latched. Once a year, check the head gasket: if the head will not grip a valve firmly, the gasket is worn and a new one costs less than a coffee.

**Troubleshooting.** If pressure will not climb, the head is not seated or the gasket is worn — reseat first, replace second. If air escapes around the valve when you remove the head, you are releasing too slowly; the lever-pop is a skill worth practising ten times. If the gauge reads zero on a Presta wheel, you have unscrewed the valve's little top nut — snug it with a fingertip and reseat. A gauge that sticks at its last reading needs a tap, and one that never moves needs replacing; calibrate your trust to the tap-and-read habit. Greased rod, tension-free hose, seated gasket — that is the whole maintenance story of this pump.
